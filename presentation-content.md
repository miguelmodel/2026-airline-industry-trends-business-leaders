# A Record-Year Forecast, Halved by Mid-Year

IATA's December 2025 outlook projected a record $41 billion net profit at a 3.9% margin.

By June 2026 that projection was roughly halved to $23 billion at a 2.0% margin.

Revenue still rose to a forecast $1.165 trillion, up 9.4%, as higher fares recouped costs.

Even so, return on invested capital of 4.3% sits well below the 8.5% cost of capital.

**Speaker Notes:**
The two 2026 figures are different forecast vintages published six months apart, not a start-and-end result; the June revision was driven principally by a sharp rise in jet-fuel and other input costs.
Note the paradox for the finance audience: revenue and yields rose while net profit fell, so 2026 is a margin story rather than a demand-collapse story.
Reinforce that even the optimistic December projection sat below the cost of capital, and the June revision widened that gap to a 4.2-point shortfall.
Transition: the demand data under this forecast tells its own two-part story.
Sources: IATA, 9 Dec 2025 (https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2025-releases/2025-12-09-01/); IATA Global Outlook, Jun 2026 (https://www.iata.org/en/publications/economics/reports/global-outlook-for-air-transport-june-2026/).

# Demand Softened, but Planes Stayed Full

Global demand grew 3.8% year on year in January 2026.

It then turned negative, falling 2.2% in May and 1.7% in June - the first year-on-year decline in more than two years.

The weakness was concentrated: excluding one region, June demand fell only 0.6%.

Passenger load factor still reached a record-tier 84.2% in June, with capacity staying constrained.

**Speaker Notes:**
Frame this as observed, realized data rather than forecast; it is the demand backdrop to the profit revision on the prior slide.
Emphasize that most markets remained resilient - international demand excluding the one soft region still rose - so this is dispersion, not a broad downturn.
The record load factor matters strategically: constrained capacity is holding utilization near all-time highs even as headline demand dips, which supports pricing.
Transition: with full planes and rising fares, growth in 2026 became value-led rather than volume-led.
Sources: IATA, 2 Mar 2026 (https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2026-releases/2026-03-02-02/); IATA APMA June 2026 (https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repository/publications/economic-reports/air-passenger-market-analysis-june-2026/); IATA, 30 Jul 2026 (https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2026-releases/07-30-air-passenger-demand-falls-june/).

# Growth Turned Value-Led and Uneven

Business-travel spend is forecast at a record $1.71 trillion in 2026, up 7.2%, while trips rise only 1.3%.

Passenger yields moved from roughly flat to a forecast plus-7% as carriers passed through higher costs.

Profitability diverged by model: European full-service carriers narrowed losses while European low-cost carriers deepened theirs despite 21% higher revenue.

Long-run demand stays intact, with Airbus projecting 3.6% annual traffic growth through 2044.

**Speaker Notes:**
Highlight the spend-versus-trips gap: value is growing faster than volume, reflecting higher spend per trip; note this business-travel figure is a single-source GBTA forecast reported via CAPA.
The model dispersion is the key executive signal - aggregate numbers hide sharp differences between regions and between full-service and low-cost operators, so business-model exposure matters more than the industry average.
Close the market section by separating the near-term cost squeeze from the intact long-run structural growth trend, which remains a forecast rather than a realized 2026 result.
Transition: the dominant swing factor behind the mid-year profit cut was fuel.

# Fuel Is the Defining 2026 Cost Shock

IATA forecasts average jet fuel at $152 per barrel in 2026, almost 70% above 2025.

The industry fuel bill rises from $252 billion to $351 billion - an increase of about $100 billion.

Fuel's share of operating costs climbs from 25.4% to 31.4%.

Consumption is essentially flat year on year, so price alone drives the increase.

**Speaker Notes:**
This is the single largest cost story of 2026 and the primary reason the profit forecast was cut; frame it as a supply-driven price event, with fuel volumes flat.
For the operations and finance audience, the cost-share jump to 31.4% is the strategic point: fuel now consumes nearly a third of operating cost, compressing the buffer against any further shocks.
Keep the description neutral and market-based - this is an energy-price and refining-margin event, described without any external cause attribution.
Transition: what makes this shock hard to manage is how fast the forecasts moved and how little of the exposure is hedged.
Sources: IATA Fuel Fact Sheet, Jun 2026 (https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repository/pressroom/fact-sheets/fact-sheet-fuel/); IATA Economics, 19 Jun 2026 (https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repository/publications/economic-reports/profits-and-margins-to-shrink-in-2026-but-remain-positive/).

# The Price Path Is Volatile and Largely Unhedged

EIA's February 2026 outlook expected Brent to fall to about $58 per barrel this year.

Its August outlook raised the 2026 figure to $87 and projected $69 for 2027 - a roughly $29 swing in six months.

A record jet crack spread near $57 per barrel means refining scarcity, not just crude, is driving jet prices.

Airlines hedged about a third of 2026 fuel globally - near 70% in Europe, minimal among North American majors - and most hedges reference crude, leaving the crack spread exposed.

**Speaker Notes:**
The point for leaders is conditionality: these forecasts assume the supply disruption eases and production recovers in early 2027, so the 2026-2027 path is unusually sensitive to whether that holds.
Explain the crack-spread nuance plainly: because most hedges are on crude rather than jet fuel, the record refining margin sits largely uncovered even for carriers that hedge heavily.
Note the regional dispersion in hedging as neutral evidence - European carriers entered 2026 far more hedged than North American majors, which changes near-term cost exposure by region.
The hedge-coverage figures are approximate and secondary: the global roughly one-third, Europe near 70%, and minimal-to-zero for North American majors come from a secondary summary of the IATA Global Outlook (The Frequent Flier, Jun 2026), with carrier-level positions compiled from company disclosures (Reuters via RTE, 3 Mar 2026); the crude-versus-jet hedge-basis point is from the IATA Fuel Fact Sheet.
Transition: fuel is the acute 2026 cost; the slower-moving constraints are on the supply of aircraft, engines, and people.
Sources: EIA, 11 Feb 2026 (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67164); EIA STEO, 11 Aug 2026 (https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/steo/); IATA Fuel Fact Sheet, Jun 2026 (https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repository/pressroom/fact-sheets/fact-sheet-fuel/); The Frequent Flier, Jun 2026, secondary IATA Global Outlook summary (https://thefrequentflier.com/briefing/iata-2026-outlook-fuel-sorting); Reuters via RTE, 3 Mar 2026, compiling carrier disclosures (https://www.rte.ie/news/business/2026/0303/1561435-airlines-hedge-against-fuel-price-hikes/).

# The Binding Constraint Moved to Aircraft Supply

Airbus delivered 793 aircraft and Boeing 600 in 2025, both improved yet still below capability.

The combined order backlog has surpassed roughly 17,000 aircraft - about 12 years of output at current build rates.

Estimates put manufacturing output about 24% below 2019, leaving roughly 5,300 fewer aircraft than the pre-pandemic trend implies.

Narrowbody build rates remain under target, at about 54 per month for the A320 family against a 75 goal and 42 for the 737 against 57.

**Speaker Notes:**
Reframe the industry's problem: through the mid-2020s the limiter shifted from filling seats to obtaining aircraft, and that constraint is slow-moving.
Clarify the distinction between production and deliveries - the "missing fleet" estimate from IATA and Cirium is corroborated independently by Oliver Wyman's production estimate, and finished airframes are sometimes parked awaiting engines.
The strategic implication for fleet and network planning: capacity discipline is being imposed by supply, which is part of what keeps load factors and asset values high.
Transition: the sharpest piece of the supply constraint is engines and the maintenance needed to keep them flying.
Sources: Airbus, 12 Jan 2026 (https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-01-airbus-reports-793-commercial-aircraft-deliveries-in-2025); Boeing FY2025 (https://s2.q4cdn.com/661678649/files/doc_financials/2025/q4/Press-Release.pdf); IATA Supply Chain Highlights (https://www.iata.org/en/iata-repository/pressroom/presentations/supply-chain-study-highlights/?pubDate=20260817); Oliver Wyman 2026-2036 (https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2026/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2026-2036.html).

# Engines and Maintenance Are a Multi-Year Strain

At the March 2025 peak, 648 next-generation single-aisle aircraft - 28% of that engine fleet - were grounded awaiting shop visits, spares, or parts.

Groundings are easing in 2026, but unevenly, with some programs expected to take two to three more years.

Annual engine shop visits are forecast to multiply by 2040, from roughly 600 to 800 toward more than 5,000 for one family.

Global maintenance demand reached $136 billion in 2025 and is projected near $193 billion by 2036, with an aging fleet flown harder at about 13 years old.

**Speaker Notes:**
This is the most disruptive fleet-availability constraint: durability issues on new single-aisle engines pulled aircraft out of service faster than shops could return them.
For operators, the forward risk is the maintenance supercycle - shop-visit demand multiplies through the 2030s and 2040s while the fleet ages, so maintenance timelines increasingly shape schedules and unit costs.
Keep the MRO and fleet-age figures labeled as provider forecasts and scope-dependent estimates; the ~13-year figure is the broad fleet average, with the passenger-only figure near 12.8 years.
The easing trajectory and timeline are program-specific and reported by trade press: manufacturers report A220 and E2 GTF issues largely resolved by end-2026, while an A320neo (PW1100G) operator quoted in AIN/Leeham expects two to three more years, so treat the recovery timeline as a manufacturer-and-operator statement rather than a settled forecast.
Transition: even with aircraft and engines available, reliability depends on people and airspace capacity.
Sources: IATA/Emerton engine-MRO study (https://www.iata.org/contentassets/85b59d951fc04c1c83fa2aab47824300/single-aisle-aircraft-engines-mro.pdf); IATA, 24 Jun 2026 (https://www.iata.org/en/pressroom/2026-releases/06-24-urgent-action-needed-to-ease-engine-mro-bottlenecks/); AIN/Leeham, 17 Jul 2026 (https://www.ainonline.com/aviation-news/air-transport/2026-07-17/pratt-whitney-nears-end-gtf-saga); Oliver Wyman 2026-2036 (https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2026/feb/global-fleet-and-mro-market-forecast-2026-2036.html).

# People and Airspace Shape Reliability

Boeing projects demand for 660,000 new pilots and 710,000 new technicians through 2044, while about 41% of US certified mechanics are already over 60.

The US certified-controller workforce stood at 13,164 at the end of fiscal 2025, about 6% below its 2015 level.

European summer punctuality improved to 71% as flow-management delays fell 27%, though delays stayed above target.

US full-year cancellations edged up to 1.5%, and European network delay costs exceeded 2 billion euros in 2025.

**Speaker Notes:**
Position workforce as a decade-long structural theme, not a one-year gap: pilots, technicians, and controllers all face long training pipelines, and the mechanic age profile signals a coming retirement wave.
On reliability, stress that Europe improved but remained constrained by controller staffing, while the US ran a record schedule with slightly higher cancellations; the two systems measure punctuality differently and are not directly comparable.
Keep the two US controller measures distinct: the 13,164 end-FY2025 headcount and its ~6% decline from 2015 are GAO figures, while a separate staffing measure puts controllers roughly 3,000 below target (CBS News); the count and the shortfall use different scopes and should not be merged.
Tie it back to cost: the European delay-cost figure quantifies how staffing and infrastructure limits convert into real operating expense.
Transition: against these physical constraints, the industry is investing heavily in digital capability.

# Record Tech Investment, Uneven Realized Value

Air transport committed a record $50.8 billion to technology in 2025, with airlines accounting for $36 billion, or 3.6% of revenue.

63% of airlines report using AI in operations control, and 79% name generative AI their top priority for the next year.

Yet a vendor-sponsored survey found only 35% had deployed AI in guest-facing uses and just 12.5% felt ready to scale.

Across the evidence, data coordination and quality - not model capability - are the binding constraint on measured value.

**Speaker Notes:**
The narrative here is a gap between ambition and deployment: intent and spend are at records, but realized, guest-facing value lags, and the 35% and 12.5% deployment figures come from a vendor-sponsored survey of 800 travel and airline professionals - a mixed travel sample - so they should be read as directional rather than precise.

# Commercial Engines Rest on a Trust Foundation

Ancillary revenue reached an estimated $157 billion in 2025, equal to 15.7% of total airline revenue.

Loyalty and co-brand economics remain durable: Delta reported its American Express relationship generated about $8 billion, roughly 10% of 2025 revenue.

Trust is the counterweight: airlines have historically drawn about 46% of online payment fraud, and 2025 saw ten major cyber incidents, including a breach exposing 5.7 million customers.

# Carbon Costs Rise Faster Than SAF Supply

EU aviation free carbon allowances were fully removed from 1 January 2026, so covered flights now buy every allowance at auction, with EU allowances trading in a market range of roughly 70 to 95 euros per tonne.

SAF mandates are live and rising, from a 2% EU blend in 2025 toward 70% by 2050, and from 2% to 10% by 2030 in the UK.

Yet 2026 SAF output is only about 2.4 million tonnes, roughly 0.8% of fuel use, at about $4.3 billion in added cost.

The synthetic e-SAF gap is starkest, with about 0.02 million tonnes of capacity against roughly 0.6 million tonnes mandated across the EU and UK by 2030.

**Speaker Notes:**
The throughline for this section is a widening gap between binding obligations and delivered capacity, most visible in carbon costs and SAF.
For finance and sustainability leaders, note that the EU ETS free-allowance phase-out turns carbon into a full cash cost from 2026, and CORSIA shifts from its voluntary first phase to mandatory participation in 2027 (per EASA), so emissions costs are now recurring line items that interact with fuel economics.
The 70 to 95 euros per tonne figure is a traded market range from secondary carbon-market reporting (euenergy.live, Apr 2026), not a fixed or official price, and should be presented as a market estimate.
Define the term for non-specialists: e-SAF (electro-SAF, or power-to-liquid) is synthetic jet fuel made from green hydrogen and captured carbon dioxide, the scarcest and most expensive SAF pathway.
Keep SAF figures labeled as estimates; the e-SAF shortfall is the binding bottleneck, with very little announced capacity having reached final investment decision.
Transition: alongside carbon, consumer rules, insurance, and climate exposure round out the resilience picture.

The EU adopted an updated air passenger rights package in July 2026, keeping the three-hour compensation threshold and adding transparency and reduced-mobility protections.

# How the 2026 Trends Connect

Demand is structurally strong and load factors are at records, yet margins are thin and highly sensitive to cost.

Fuel is the largest 2026 shock, consuming nearly a third of operating cost and driving the mid-year profit cut.

Supply-side limits in aircraft, engines, labor, and airspace now shape capacity, unit cost, and reliability more than short-run demand.

Digital investment is at records but its value is gated by data coordination, while carbon and consumer obligations rise faster than SAF supply and delivered capacity.
